Cancer Biology Series

Part 0: Cancer Disease Burden

Background to Cancer

Cancer is not just a disease of cells that causes deterioration of the normal functioning of the human body but a disease that affects families, communities, and nations. Around the world, cancer has become one of the leading causes of illness and death, claiming millions of lives every year. According to the World Health Organization, cancer accounted for nearly 10 million deaths in 2020 alone, roughly one in every six deaths globally (WHO AFRO, 2022).

While these numbers are staggering, what is even more concerning is the rising trend of cancer incidence in low- and middle-income regions, particularly in Africa. Once considered a disease of high-income nations, cancer is increasingly taking root across the African continent, where limited diagnostic resources, delayed detection, and inadequate public awareness continue to fuel late-stage presentations and preventable deaths (IUCC, 2023).

In Ghana and many other African countries, cancers such as breast, cervical, prostate, and liver cancer are among the most frequently diagnosed. Yet, many people still underestimate their risk or associate cancer solely with developed nations. This misconception delays prevention, screening, and timely treatment. At the end of the day this contributes to worsening health outcomes and stretching already burdened healthcare systems (IUCC, 2023).

Understanding the true burden of cancer is not just a scientific necessity but also a social responsibility. Public education is the foundation for change. When people know the facts, they are empowered to seek early screening, demand better healthcare infrastructure, and support ongoing research and advocacy.

Addressing the cancer burden begins with one essential question: “Why should we care?”

The answer is simple. Behind every statistic is a person, a family, and a story — and every early detection or prevention effort can save a life.

The National Picture

According to the GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates, Ghana records approximately:

  • 27,385 new cancer cases every year
  • 17,944 deaths annually
  • 63,764 people living with cancer within the last five years

These numbers reflect not only the growing cancer incidence but also the high fatality rate indicating that nearly 65% of cancer patients in Ghana die each year (Sung et al., 2021). The leading types of cancers in Ghana are breast cancer, liver cancer, cervical cancer, prostate cancer, and non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (WHO).

Cancer risk in Ghana is influenced by a combination of biological, environmental, and health system factors:

  • Infections: Hepatitis B/C (liver cancer), HPV (cervical cancer)
  • Environmental exposure: Aflatoxins from contaminated foods
  • Lifestyle changes: Diet, alcohol, inactivity
  • Healthcare gaps: Late diagnosis and limited oncology infrastructure

Rural and low-income populations are particularly vulnerable due to lack of screening and awareness (GHS, 2023).

Part 1: Foundations of Cancer

What is Cancer?

Cancer is a group of diseases characterized by uncontrolled cell growth and the ability of these abnormal cells to invade surrounding tissues and metastasize to distant organs. Unlike normal cells, cancer cells evade growth control signals, resist cell death, and sustain proliferation (Piña-Sánchez et al., 2021). Cancer cells acquire capabilities known as the hallmarks of cancer (Hanahan & Weinberg, 2011).

  • Sustaining proliferative signaling
  • Evading growth suppressors
  • Resisting cell death
  • Inducing angiogenesis
  • Enabling replicative immortality
  • Activating invasion and metastasis
  • Reprogramming cellular metabolism
  • Evading immune destruction

These features together make cancer cells aggressive, hard to control, and capable of spreading (Hanahan, 2022; Hanahan & Weinberg, 2011).

Cell Cycle and its Regulation

a. Overview of the Cell Cycle

The cell cycle includes G1, S, G2, and M phases. Cells may also pause in G0. In S, DNA replicates. In G2, cells prepare for mitosis. In M, karyokinesis and cytokinesis create two identical daughter cells (Malumbres & Barbacid, 2009; Vermeulen et al., 2003).

b. Key Regulatory Proteins and Complexes

Cyclin–CDK complexes drive progression: Cyclin D–CDK4/6 (G1), Cyclin E–CDK2 (G1/S), Cyclin A–CDK2 (S), Cyclin B–CDK1 (G2/M) (Dombrádi et al., 2002; Malumbres & Barbacid, 2009).

Checkpoints in the Cell Cycle

  • G1/S: DNA integrity check; p53→p21 inhibits CDKs; Rb restrains E2F (Molinari, 2000; Sherr & McCormick, 2002).
  • Intra-S: Pauses DNA synthesis after damage (Molinari, 2000).
  • G2/M: Ensures replication completion; CHK1/CHK2 involved (Molinari, 2000).

Tumor Suppressors and Oncogenes

  • p53: Cell cycle arrest, senescence, apoptosis (Osada & Takahashi, 2002).
  • Rb: Blocks premature S-phase entry via E2F (Sherr & McCormick, 2002).
  • Cyclin D1: Amplified in several cancers (Osada & Takahashi, 2002; Sanchez-Vega et al., 2018).
  • CDK4/6: Often activated in glioblastoma and melanoma (Sanchez-Vega et al., 2018).

Cell Cycle Dysregulation in Cancer

  • Loss of p53 in >50% of cancers (Sherr & McCormick, 2002).
  • Overactive CDK4/6 hyper-phosphorylates Rb, pushing G1→S.
  • Excess Cyclin D accelerates G1 in several tumors (Malumbres & Barbacid, 2009).
