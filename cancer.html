Cancer Biology Series

Part 0: Cancer Disease Burden

Background to Cancer

Cancer is not just a disease of cells that causes deterioration of the normal functioning of the human body but a disease that affects families, communities, and nations. Around the world, cancer has become one of the leading causes of illness and death, claiming millions of lives every year. According to the World Health Organization, cancer accounted for nearly 10 million deaths in 2020 alone, roughly one in every six deaths globally (WHO AFRO, 2022).

While these numbers are staggering, what is even more concerning is the rising trend of cancer incidence in low- and middle-income regions, particularly in Africa. Once considered a disease of high-income nations, cancer is increasingly taking root across the African continent, where limited diagnostic resources, delayed detection, and inadequate public awareness continue to fuel late-stage presentations and preventable deaths (IUCC, 2023).

In Ghana and many other African countries, cancers such as breast, cervical, prostate, and liver cancer are among the most frequently diagnosed. Yet, many people still underestimate their risk or associate cancer solely with developed nations. This misconception delays prevention, screening, and timely treatment. At the end of the day this contributes to worsening health outcomes and stretching already burdened healthcare systems (IUCC, 2023).

Understanding the true burden of cancer is not just a scientific necessity but also a social responsibility. Public education is the foundation for change. When people know the facts, they are empowered to seek early screening, demand better healthcare infrastructure, and support ongoing research and advocacy.

Addressing the cancer burden begins with one essential question: “Why should we care?”

The answer is simple! This is because behind every statistic is a person, a family, and a story — and every early detection or prevention effort can save a life.

The National Picture

According to the GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates, Ghana records approximately:

  • 27,385 new cancer cases every year
  • 17,944 deaths annually
  • 63,764 people living with cancer within the last five years

These numbers reflect not only the growing cancer incidence but also the high fatality rate indicating that nearly 65% of cancer patients in Ghana die each year (Sung et al., 2021). The leading types of cancers in Ghana are breast cancer, liver cancer, cervical cancer, prostate cancer, and non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (WHO).

Cancer risk in Ghana is influenced by a combination of biological, environmental, and health system factors:

  • Infections: Hepatitis B/C (liver cancer), HPV (cervical cancer)
  • Environmental exposure: Aflatoxins from contaminated foods
  • Lifecycle changes: Diet, alcohol, inactivity
  • Healthcare gaps: Late diagnosis and limited oncology infrastructure

Rural and low-income populations are particularly vulnerable due to lack of screening and awareness (GHS, 2023).

Part 1: Foundations of Cancer

What is Cancer?

Cancer is a group of diseases characterized by uncontrolled cell growth and the ability of these abnormal cells to invade surrounding tissues and metastasize to distant organs. Unlike normal cells, cancer cells evade growth control signals, resist cell death, and sustain proliferation (Piña-Sánchez et al., 2021). Cancer cells acquire capabilities known as the hallmarks of cancer, including sustaining proliferative signaling, evading growth suppressors, resisting cell death, enabling replicative immortality, inducing angiogenesis, and activating invasion and metastasis (Hanahan & Weinberg, 2011).

  • Sustaining proliferative signaling: Cancer cells grow and divide without depending on normal growth signals.
  • Evading growth suppressors: They ignore signals that usually slow down or stop cell division.
  • Resisting cell death: They avoid apoptosis, which normally removes damaged or unneeded cells.
  • Inducing angiogenesis: They form new blood vessels to supply oxygen and nutrients to the tumor.
  • Enabling replicative immortality: They divide indefinitely, unlike normal cells that stop after a certain point.
  • Activating invasion and metastasis: They spread to other parts of the body beyond the original site.
  • Reprogramming cellular metabolism: They change their metabolism to support continuous growth.
  • Evading immune destruction: They hide from or resist attacks by the immune system.

These features work together to make cancer cells aggressive, hard to control, and capable of spreading in the body (Hanahan, 2022; Hanahan & Weinberg, 2011).

Cell Cycle and its Regulation

a. Overview of the Cell Cycle

The cell cycle is a series of stages that a cell goes through as it grows and divides. It begins with the G1 phase (Gap 1), where the cell grows and actively synthesizes proteins and organelles. During this phase, the cell also prepares for DNA replication. At a critical decision point, the cell either continues into the next phase (S phase) or exits the cycle and enters a resting state known as the G0 phase. In G0, the cell stops dividing. Some cells, like neurons, remain in G0 permanently, while others, such as liver cells, may reenter the cycle when needed (Malumbres & Barbacid, 2009; Vermeulen et al., 2003).

If the cell continues through the cycle, it enters the S phase (Synthesis phase), where DNA replication occurs and each chromosome is duplicated. After DNA synthesis, the cell enters the G2 phase (Gap 2). In this phase, the cell prepares for mitosis by continuing to grow and producing additional proteins and organelles (Malumbres & Barbacid, 2009; Vermeulen et al., 2003).

Finally, the cell enters the M phase (Mitosis phase). This phase involves the division of the nucleus, called karyokinesis, followed by the division of the cytoplasm, known as cytokinesis. The result is two genetically identical daughter cells, each ready to begin a new cycle (Malumbres & Barbacid, 2009; Vermeulen et al., 2003).

b. Key Regulatory Proteins and Complexes

The cell cycle is tightly regulated by specific proteins known as cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs). CDKs are enzymes that become active only when bound to particular cyclins. These cyclin–CDK complexes are responsible for driving the cell through the different phases of the cycle by phosphorylating target proteins that promote progression (Dombrádi et al., 2002).

Each phase of the cycle is regulated by a distinct cyclin–CDK pair:

  • Cyclin D–CDK4/6 controls progression through the G1 phase
  • Cyclin E–CDK2 regulates the critical G1/S transition
  • Cyclin A–CDK2 promotes progression through the S phase
  • Cyclin B–CDK1 is responsible for the G2/M transition

Together, these complexes ensure the orderly and accurate replication and division of cells (Malumbres & Barbacid, 2009).

Checkpoints in the Cell Cycle

Checkpoints are control mechanisms that ensure each stage of the cell cycle is completed correctly before the cell moves forward. The main checkpoints are:

1. G1/S Checkpoint

  • Ensures that the DNA is not damaged before replication begins (Molinari, 2000).
  • p53 activates p21, which blocks CDKs if damage is detected (Sherr & McCormick, 2002).
  • Rb protein holds back E2F when in its hypophosphorylated form, preventing the cell from progressing (Sherr & McCormick, 2002).

2. Intra-S Checkpoint

  • Temporarily pauses DNA synthesis in response to DNA damage (Molinari, 2000).

3. G2/M Checkpoint

  • Confirms that all DNA has been correctly replicated before mitosis begins (Molinari, 2000).
  • Involves regulation by CHK1/CHK2 kinases (Molinari, 2000).

Tumor Suppressors and Oncogenes in Cell Cycle Regulation

a. Tumor Suppressors

  • p53: Called the “guardian of the genome,” it can pause the cell cycle, trigger cell aging (senescence), or cause cell death (apoptosis) when there’s DNA damage (Osada & Takahashi, 2002).
  • Rb: Stops the cell from entering the S phase too early by holding back a protein called E2F (Sherr & McCormick, 2002).

b. Oncogenes

  • Cyclin D1: Often found in high amounts in breast cancer and lymphomas (Osada & Takahashi, 2002; Sanchez-Vega et al., 2018).
  • CDK4/6: Frequently increased in glioblastomas and melanomas, speeding up cell division (Sanchez-Vega et al., 2018).

Cell Cycle Dysregulation in Cancer

In cancer, the cell cycle checkpoints are often turned off or don’t work properly. This means cells can keep dividing even when there’s DNA damage or chromosome problems (Glaviano et al., 2025). Some common examples include:

  • Loss of p53: This happens in more than 50% of human cancers. Without p53, damaged cells don’t stop or die—they keep multiplying (Sherr & McCormick, 2002).
  • Overactive CDK4/6: These proteins can cause too much phosphorylation of Rb, allowing the cell to move into the S phase too early (Sherr & McCormick, 2002).
  • Excess Cyclin D: Leads to continuous cell growth by speeding up the G1 phase and is seen in several cancers like breast cancer and lymphomas (Malumbres & Barbacid, 2009).
